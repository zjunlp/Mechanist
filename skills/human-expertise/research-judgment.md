# Research judgment

## Worthwhile questions

Specific means testable, not tiny. Identify the expert prior and what a result adds: direction, magnitude, mechanism, boundary, or genuinely different setting. Compare nearest findings, not just topics.

Who changes a decision if the hypothesis is true? Who changes one if false? High-stakes framing alone is not a contribution. Simple methods and informative negative results can matter.

## Discriminating experiments

Name alternatives, including decoding, tokenization, formatting, length, leakage, and evaluator artifacts. Pair consequential rivals with controls that actually distinguish them. Prefer decisive comparisons over long lists of weak baselines.

For each ablation, ask what it tests, what outcomes mean, and whether capacity or compute confounds it. Identify no-op changes. Exploratory runs can be useful because they reveal new questions rather than defend existing claims.

Stress-test plausible failure conditions. Model, dataset, and method swaps are useful when informative, not because every axis must be filled. Incomparable tests do not become a robustness proof by majority vote.

## Refinement and interpretation

Keep the actual question in view when responding to criticism. Compare minimal adequate routes and reuse suitable components. Avoid contribution sprawl or fashionable modules added to improve a review score. Another model's confidence is advice, not evidence.

Make proposed methods concrete at the points that determine feasibility: the internal objects, data, interfaces, interventions or losses, measurements, and resource assumptions. A module name alone is not an implementable method. Distinguish a decisive experiment from a useful extension; do not let a growing experiment menu replace a clear comparison.

Separate implementation failure, unreliable measurement, inapplicable methods, and unsupported hypotheses. Repairing one need not rewrite all others. Inconclusive measurements must not become a permanent refuge for a favored claim.

## Expression

Distinguish plans from observations. Make the final account self-contained: define conditions, units, models, populations, and comparisons. Keep important quantities consistent. Prefer numbers to vague scope claims. State alternatives, boundaries, and negative findings. Let document and figure conventions serve the audience rather than a universal publication template.
